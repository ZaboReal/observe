    One seat. Two drivers.

    Most SaaS is sold by the seat: one login, priced for one person working part of the day. AI agents now use that same login, sometimes alongside the person and often long after they've logged off. Your pricing can't see the difference. Observe can.

    What's happening

    Morgan has one seat. They work weekdays, about seven hours a day. Claude in Chrome uses Morgan's login too: it clears the inbox at 6 am, exports reports at night and keeps going at the weekend. To your billing system, that's one very busy user.

    What we measure

    For every login and account: hours driven by the person and hours driven by agents, the actions each took, which agents were used, and every hand-off between them.

    Why it matters

    Your costs follow usage: servers, data and support. Revenue stays flat per seat. A team of ten can buy three seats and let agents do the rest, and nobody notices until margins move.

    Common questions.

    Still have one? Write to us. A founder will read it.

    What counts as an AI agent?

    Anything that drives your product for a person: browser extensions like Claude in Chrome, AI browsers like Comet, agents built into Chrome and Edge, cloud agents like ChatGPT agent, and automation frameworks. We recognise 70 by name; anything else that is clearly automated is still caught as unknown automation.

    How is this different from bot protection?

    Bot protection asks whether this is a bot; your login asks whether this is the right person. An in-browser agent passes both: it runs on your customer's own device, with their cookies and their account. We answer the question that's left: which agent is acting, for whom, and is it allowed?

    What do you collect?

    How the page is driven: pointer paths, click and key timing, how text and scrolling arrive, and markers agents leave on the page. Never what people type or see, and no screenshots.

    Will it get in my users' way?

    Nothing is blocked unless you write a rule that blocks it. Most teams start by seeing which agents are there, then add rules for two or three sensitive actions.

    How do you recognise agents that don't identify themselves?

    Many leave exact markers on the page. For the rest, a decision model reads how the session is driven and compares it with what we know about each agent. Agents that sign their requests are verified cryptographically.

    Is this only about stopping bad agents?

    No. Most agent use is your customers getting work done. Rules let you give good agents a smooth path, see which features they rely on, and price their use fairly, while keeping risky actions behind your customers' approval.
